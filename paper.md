# Who was Bodhidharma? A dated-witness stratigraphy and lineage-chart forensics

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

Bodhidharma is treated here as a dated-witness problem: a meditation master who exists almost entirely inside Chinese records is examined by stratigraphy — witnesses ordered by their own dates, each motif assigned to its first dated appearance, and the lineage charts cross-dated node by node with the generational arithmetic shown. The result is a small historical kernel and a large, datable accretion. A real master from the western regions flourished c. 480–530, was present in Luoyang in 516–526, and was dead before 534; the earliest witness, Yang Xuanzhi (c. 547), calls him a Persian/Central Asian, the next biographical layers call him South Indian and disagree with each other on his caste and pedigree, and no Indian source attests the name Bodhidharma at all. The famous 527 arrival fails three independent tests — against the Luoyang sighting, against the death of Guṇabhadra in 468, and against its own text's Song Yun story — while the earlier Liu Song arrival, by the 470s at the latest, is sustainable at the price of a long life. Almost everything else in the received biography enters centuries later: the Emperor Wu dialogue first appears c. 758, Huike's self-severed arm is first attested epigraphically in 798 against Daoxuan's earlier account of an arm lost to bandits, the nine years at Shaolin are canonised in 1004, and the martial-arts link rests on a text of 1624. His supposed teacher Prajñātāra is absent from Tanlin, from Daoxuan and from the *Lengjia Shizi Ji*, is first attested in 801 in the *Baolin Zhuan*'s 28-patriarch list, and stands at exactly the seam where an earlier list, the *Fu Fazang Yinyuan Zhuan* (472), had closed the transmission at Siṃha.

**Keywords:** Bodhidharma; Chan; lineage; source stratigraphy; Prajñātāra; Laṅkāvatāra; Shaolin; hagiography

## 1. Introduction

The received biography of Bodhidharma is familiar: an Indian prince-monk, taught by Prajñātāra, who arrives in China in 527, tells Emperor Wu that his temple-building has earned no merit, crosses the Yangtze on a reed, sits facing a wall for nine years near Shaolin, accepts Huike after Huike severs his own arm in the snow, and after his death is met carrying a single sandal, his tomb found to contain one shoe. This paper asks which parts of that biography are carried by witnesses close to the man's life, and which parts enter the record later — and how much later, in dated layers.

The method is stratigraphy by dated witness. Witnesses are ordered by the witness's own date, not by the date of the events they describe. A motif's first dated appearance is its terminus, not its truth: it establishes when a claim can first be shown in the record, and the lag between a death before 534 and a motif first attested in 758, 952 or 1004 is itself evidence about the kind of claim it is. The lineage charts are treated the same way, as documents to be cross-dated: each node is checked for an independent date, and the intervals between teacher and pupil are computed rather than assumed.

The evidence base is the primary witnesses as reported in the standard scholarship — Broughton (1999), McRae, Faure, and Shahar (2008) among them — and dated epigraphic and catalogue facts. Two gaps are declared in §10 and qualify the whole paper: the exact first dated occurrence of the epithet “blue-eyed barbarian” was not pinned in this build, and the *Baolin Zhuan* (801), which fixed the 28-patriarch list, is lost and known through quotation, so anything credited to it is one notch softer than an extant text.

The headline result can be stated in one sentence. A real meditation master from the western regions was present in Luoyang between 516 and 526 and dead before 534; almost everything else in the famous biography — the 527 arrival, the emperor, the nine years at Shaolin, the severed arm, the single shoe, and his teacher Prajñātāra — enters the record between 250 and 500 years after his death.

## 2. The witnesses in date order

### 2.1 Yang Xuanzhi, *Luoyang Qielan Ji*, compiled 547 — the earliest witness

A gazetteer of the monasteries of Luoyang, written about the city as it was before its destruction. In the Yongning Temple entry, Yang Xuanzhi mentions a monk named Bodhidharma in one breath and moves on. What he says, exactly:

- **Origin label:** a “hu person from the Persian country” — *hu* is the generic Chinese term for peoples of the Western Regions / Central Asia; the label is “Persian Central Asian,” not “Indian prince.” The earliest witness (Yang Xuanzhi, c. 547) calls him a Persian/Central Asian.
- **Where seen:** at Yongning Temple, Luoyang, admiring its great stūpa. Yongning Temple was built in 516 and burned in 534, so the sighting falls inside 516–526 on Broughton's reading: the visit must precede the temple's destruction, and Yang's account describes the intact temple.
- **Age claim:** Bodhidharma says of himself, “I am 150 years old, and I have passed through numerous countries.” The 150-year age is therefore the earliest motif in the entire dossier — present at the first mention, as a self-claim, in a source that treats him as a wonder-working foreign monk.
- **What is not here:** no meditation teaching, no disciples, no lineage, no Shaolin, no emperor, no India beyond the western-regions label, no teacher.

### 2.2 Tanlin (506–574), preface to the *Two Entrances and Four Practices* — the earliest doctrinal attribution

Tanlin was a near-contemporary — more plausibly a student of Huike than of Bodhidharma himself. His short preface, preserved in Dunhuang manuscripts and quoted in the *Lengjia Shizi Ji*, is the first text to attach a teaching and a pedigree to the name:

- **Origin:** South Indian, third son of a great king. The royal pedigree enters here, in the generation after the man's death, and already conflicts with Yang's “Persian” label and, as §2.3 shows, with Daoxuan's caste.
- **Doctrine:** the *Two Entrances and Four Practices* is attributed to Bodhidharma, with *biguan* (壁觀, “wall contemplation”) named as his mind-pacifying method. *Biguan* here is the name of a contemplative method, not yet nine years facing a cave wall at Shaolin.
- **Disciples:** the first to name any — Daoyu and Huike.
- **What is not here:** no teacher of Bodhidharma is named. No Prajñātāra. No arrival date. No emperor. No arm, no shoe, no Shaolin.

### 2.3 Daoxuan (596–667), *Xu Gaoseng Zhuan*, compiled c. 645, extended to 667 — the first biography

Daoxuan is a historian working from records, a century after the fact, and his entry is the first actual biography:

- **Origin:** South Indian of Brahmin stock — flatly different from Tanlin's royal, Kshatriya-implying pedigree. The two earliest biographical notices already disagree about who he was.
- **Itinerary:** he landed at Nanyue (the Guangzhou coast) during the Liu Song period — that is, arrival no later than 479 — and travelled north into Wei territory. This is the earlier of the two arrival traditions in the sources, and it is the one the arithmetic can sustain (§5).
- **Teaching:** he transmitted the four-fascicle Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra to Huike. The Laṅkāvatāra affiliation is fixed here, in the mid-7th century.
- **Huike's arm:** in Daoxuan, Huike lost his arm to bandits, and cauterised the stump himself. The self-severing in the snow does not exist yet, and contradicts the earliest biography when it arrives.
- **Death:** Bodhidharma died and was buried by the Luo River — the burial is carried in the Huike material; in Bodhidharma's own entry his end is left effectively open. The death necessarily falls before 534, the collapse of the Northern Wei world Daoxuan describes, with the Heyin massacre of 528 among the candidate contexts. Secondary summaries of Daoxuan's entry also transmit an age figure even larger than 150 (“over 180”); that figure is flagged here as a secondary-source detail.
- **What is not here:** no Prajñātāra, no Emperor Wu, no Shaolin, no nine years, no shoe.

### 2.4 The lineage is invented: Faru's epitaph (689) and the early-8th-century charts

- **Epitaph for Faru (638–689), 689:** the first lineage claim of any kind — Bodhidharma → Huike → Sengcan → Daoxin → Hongren → Faru. Before 689, no source arranges these men as a succession at all.
- ***Chuan Fabao Ji* (Du Fei, c. 710–713):** makes Bodhidharma “first patriarch” of a Chinese line.
- ***Lengjia Shizi Ji* (Jingjue, 683–750; c. 713–716):** the lineage chart proper — and it is a chart of Laṅkāvatāra masters, rooted not at Bodhidharma but at Guṇabhadra, the translator, running Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma → Huike → Sengcan → Daoxin → Hongren → Shenxiu. Jingjue's scheme serves his own teacher Xuanze's claim to the succession. It preserves Tanlin's preface, the main channel for §2.2. The mind-pacifying dialogue (“Bring me your mind and I will pacify it”) belongs to this early-8th-century stratum. Guṇabhadra is the chart's root; Prajñātāra is not in it. Jingjue, who had every motive to give Bodhidharma an Indian teacher, names none.

### 2.5 The Shenhui stratum and the Song compilations

- **Emperor Wu dialogue** (“What merit have I gained?” — “None whatsoever”; “Who stands before me?” — “I don't know”): first recorded c. 758, in an appendix to a text of the Shenhui tradition — over two centuries after the supposed audience, in the literature of a faction, Shenhui's, that was then fighting a lineage war and needed Bodhidharma staged as the anti-establishment founder. The Emperor Wu dialogue first appears c. 758.
- ***Baolin Zhuan* (801):** fixes the canonical list of 28 Indian patriarchs, ending …Prajñātāra (27) → Bodhidharma (28), and builds the Indian biographies to match (§7). Largely lost; known by quotation.
- ***Zutang Ji* (952):** the first extant text with the full traditional package assembled: arrival in 527 after a three-year sea journey; disciple of Prajñātāra; the Emperor Wu encounter; death aged 150; burial on Mt. Xiong'er; Song Yun meets him in the Pamirs three years after the burial, carrying a single sandal; the tomb is opened and contains one shoe. The 527 arrival directly contradicts Daoxuan's Liu Song arrival: the later text wins in popular memory, the earlier text wins on evidence.
- ***Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (1004):** the canonical fixing of everything: arrival dated to the 21st day of the 9th month of Putong 8 (527); the reed crossing of the Yangtze; nine years wall-gazing in a cave near Shaolin; Huike standing in the snow and cutting off his own arm; the four disciples graded skin / flesh / bone / marrow (Daofu, the nun Zongchi, Daoyu, Huike); six poisoning attempts; the original name Bodhitāra, renamed Bodhidharma by Prajñātāra; the gem test and the 67-year prophecy (§7.2). This is the biography the world knows, and it was compiled about 470 years after the man's death.
- **Shaolin, epigraphically (Shahar 2008):** a stele of 728 is the earliest evidence placing Bodhidharma in residence on Mt. Song; a stele of 798 already knows Huike's severed arm, as devotion, replacing Daoxuan's bandits. Huike's arm was lost to bandits in Daoxuan (645); the self-severing is first attested on a stele of 798. The first explicit Bodhidharma–Shaolin Temple association in a canonical text is the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (1004). The connection is real by the 8th century; it is unattested for the first two centuries.
- **Martial arts:** the link is the *Yijin Jing*, written in 1624 by the Daoist Zining with forged prefaces attributing it to Bodhidharma. It was debunked by Tang Hao in the 1930s and definitively by Shahar (2008), and popularised by the *Lao Can* novel serialisation of 1904–1907. The Shaolin-kung-fu-founder claim is a 17th-century forgery with a 20th-century marketing career.

## 4. Lineage-chart forensics: every node cross-dated

| Node | Dates | Independent cross-date? |
|---|---|---|
| Guṇabhadra | 394–468 | **Yes.** Arrived Guangzhou 435; translated the Laṅkāvatāra 443; Saṃyuktāgama 435–443. Death 468 from the biographical/catalogue tradition. The chart's one fully anchored Indian node — anchored by Chinese records of his work in China. |
| Bodhidharma | ? | **No independent dates.** All termini come from the witnesses of §2: in Luoyang 516–526 (Yang), dead before 534 (Daoxuan's world). |
| Huike | 487–593 | Partially. Daoxuan attests him as Bodhidharma's disciple and Laṅkāvatāra recipient; death in 593 per the biographical tradition. The implied lifespan of 107 years is longevity inflation of the same kind as his teacher's 150. |
| Sengcan | d. 606 | **Almost nothing independent.** His historicity as Huike's disciple is the chart's weakest link; Whalen Lai flags it as tenuous. A death year with no life around it. |
| Daoxin | 580–651 | Better: attested in near-contemporary records; the East Mountain community is real. |
| Hongren | 601–674 (some sources 602–675) | **Yes.** Multiple independent notices; his disciple Faru's epitaph (689) is a dated inscription from inside the community. The first node after Guṇabhadra where cross-dating fully works. |

The chart has two halves with opposite evidentiary characters.

The **Chinese half** — Guṇabhadra's China activity through to Hongren — is cross-datable from 435 onward, because these men worked inside the Chinese record system: catalogues, inscriptions, court history. Guṇabhadra's node is firm; Hongren's is firm; the middle is thin but not fabricated wholesale.

The **Indian half** — Mahākāśyapa through to Prajñātāra and Bodhidharma — is cross-datable nowhere. A handful of its names (Aśvaghoṣa, Nāgārjuna, Vasubandhu) are independently datable people, via Chinese translation termini and, for Vasubandhu, Paramārtha's *Life* — but their sequence positions and teacher–pupil links are unverifiable, and the final three names before Bodhidharma (Basiasita, Punyamitra, Prajñātāra) are attested nowhere outside the Chan list tradition at all (§7).

The seam between the halves is exactly Bodhidharma himself — the one figure who stands in both worlds and is documented in only one of them.

### 4.1 The generational arithmetic downstream

The chart buys continuity with longevity at two consecutive nodes: Bodhidharma at 150, Huike at 107. After Huike (d. 593), Sengcan dies only 13 years later (606), while Daoxin — born 580 — was 13 when Huike died and at most 26 when Sengcan died; his traditional nine years of service under Sengcan must fall c. 597–606, aged 17–26. That is survivable, but only just, and only because Sengcan is a near-blank. The first normal generational interval in the entire chart is Daoxin (b. 580) → Hongren (b. 601): 21 years — arriving exactly where documentation thickens.

## 5. The two arrival traditions tested

### 5.1 Tradition (a) — Liu Song arrival (Daoxuan, 645): arrival ≤ 479

- If the *Lengjia* chart's Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma link is a personal discipleship, he must reach China before Guṇabhadra's death in 468 — so arrival by the 460s, in the first half of the Liu Song window.
- Luoyang sighting 516–526: compatible. Death before 534: compatible.
- Cost: if he arrived as a trained master aged at least 30 by c. 468, he was born no later than c. 438 and died aged at least 96. Tight, but humanly possible.
- **Verdict: sustainable, at the price of a long life.** The tradition's own 150-year claim is applied at exactly this seam — the legend patches the precise joint where the arithmetic strains.

### 5.2 Tradition (b) — Liang arrival, 527 (*Zutang Ji* 952 / *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* 1004)

The 527 arrival fails three independent tests:

1. **The Yang Xuanzhi terminus.** Yang, writing in 547, places Bodhidharma at Yongning Temple in Luoyang, a temple built in 516 and destroyed in 534; the sighting falls in 516–526. A man cannot be sightseeing in Luoyang in 516–526 and land in the far south in 527. The whole tradition-(b) timeline starts after the earliest witness has already seen him in the north.
2. **The Guṇabhadra joint.** A personal disciple of Guṇabhadra (d. 468) who arrives in 527 has a gap of at least 59 years between the end of his discipleship and his arrival; on any normal training age — a disciple completing training in his 20s to 40s by 468 — he arrives aged roughly 85–110 and dies, on the traditional dating, older still. Tradition (b) is arithmetically survivable only inside the legend's own 150-year lifespan: it does not merely prefer the miracle, it requires it. The escape hatch — reading Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma as succession to an office, the Laṅkāvatāra mastership, rather than personal pupilage — saves the chart by conceding the point: an office-succession is precisely the retrojected lineage label McRae describes (§8).
3. **The Song Yun anachronism, internal to the legend.** The *Zutang Ji* itself dates the arrival to 527 and tells of Song Yun meeting the sandal-carrying Bodhidharma in the Pamirs “three years after his burial.” Song Yun's mission is independently dated 518–522. So on the legend's own terms the burial falls by c. 519 at the latest — at least eight years before the arrival the same text asserts. The legend borrows a real, dated envoy and wrecks its own chronology doing it.

## 7. Prajñātāra — the node before him

If Bodhidharma's dossier is thin, his teacher's is thinner by an order of magnitude, and the thinness is diagnostic.

### 7.1 Attestation sweep

Checked against every pre-801 witness in §2 and against the earlier Chinese lineage construction:

- **Tanlin (6th c.):** names no teacher of Bodhidharma. Absent.
- **Daoxuan (645–667):** no teacher named. Absent.
- ***Lengjia Shizi Ji* (c. 713–716):** the chart runs Guṇabhadra → Bodhidharma. Jingjue needed an Indian pedigree and used the translator instead. Prajñātāra is absent from the very chart cross-dated in §4 — he enters a different, later chart, the 28-patriarch list, not the Laṅkāvatāra chart.
- ***Fu Fazang Yinyuan Zhuan* (presented as a translation by Kekaya with Tanyao, 472):** the earlier Chinese patriarch construction — 23 patriarchs from the Buddha to Siṃha Bhikṣu, whose beheading in Kashmir is said to end the transmission altogether. Prajñātāra is not in it; worse for the later claim, the text's whole point is that there is nobody after Siṃha.
- **First appearance: the *Baolin Zhuan* (801)** fixes the 28-patriarch list with Prajñātāra at 27. The text is lost; the list is known through the tradition it standardised. First extant narratives naming him as Bodhidharma's teacher: *Zutang Ji* (952). First full biography: *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (1004), ch. 2.
- **Outside Chan lineage literature: nothing.** No Indian source, no Tibetan source — Tibetan patriarch lists follow the Fu Fazang-type line and stop at Siṃha — no Central Asian source, and no non-Chan Chinese source attests Prajñātāra the patriarch. His entire existence in the record is inside one genre, Chan lineage literature, and inside it only after 801.
- **One namesake caution:** a Sanskrit-writing Prajñātāra of Nālandā is recorded in the mid-9th century — three folios preserved in Japan, brought by Enchin — four centuries too late, with no connection shown, and a warning against name-matching. Philologists have also suggested the re-Sanskritisation itself may be wrong: the Chinese 般若多羅 has been read as Prajñādhara. Even the man's Sanskrit name is a back-formation.

### 7.2 The story layers, dated

| Motif | First dated appearance |
|---|---|
| Name in the list, as 27th patriarch | 801, *Baolin Zhuan* (lost; via the standardised list) |
| Named as Bodhidharma's teacher in an extant text | 952, *Zutang Ji* |
| His own hagiography: East-Indian Brahmin orphan, childhood name Keyura (“Precious Necklace”), recognised by Punyamitra (26th) as an incarnation of Mahāsthāmaprāpta | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 2 (presumably Baolin-derived) |
| The Kāñcī gem test: King Xiangzhi (“Most Fragrant”) of South India gifts Prajñātāra a priceless jewel; Prajñātāra tests the three princes with it; only the youngest, Bodhitāra, answers that a worldly jewel cannot illuminate itself and is surpassed by the Dharma-treasure | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* (cited to ch. 2). Keizan's *Denkōroku* (c. 1300–1320s) renames the king Siṃha and supplies the brothers' names — the story is still growing in the 14th century |
| The renaming: “Dharma means great penetration; you shall be Bodhidharma” | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 27 |
| The prophecy: “Remain in South India. After my nirvana, wait sixty-seven years, then go to China… When you go, do not dwell in the south” (with a calamity verse) | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* ch. 27 |
| His death at 67, ascending into the sky and bursting into flame, raining relics | 1004, *Jingde Chuandeng Lu* |
| Prophecies attributed to him by still later patriarchs: the 574–577 persecution; the future rise of Mazu Daoyi | Song lamp-record attributions — the dead patriarch keeps issuing prophecies as later centuries need them |

The engineering in the 67-year prophecy deserves notice. It is reverse-fitted: it exists to weld the Indian half of the chart to the Chinese dates — stay in India a precisely counted interval, then arrive on schedule. Modern devotional tradition completes the weld by supplying Prajñātāra's death as 457 CE — and 457 + 67 = 524, inside the traditional arrival window. A prophecy whose numbers only work backwards is not a prediction; it is a patch, of the same kind as the 150-year lifespan at the Guṇabhadra joint.

### 7.3 The Indian patriarch list: construction and the seam

- The *Fu Fazang* list (472) gives 23 patriarchs, Buddha → Siṃha, and closes the line with Siṃha's execution. Tiantai adopted the same 23 and appended its own Chinese masters (Huiwen, Huisi, Zhiyi).
- Chan needed the line open: Bodhidharma had to receive something in India to bring to China. The *Baolin Zhuan* (801) therefore (i) inserts Vasumitra into the early list (23 → 24), and (ii) reopens the line the *Fu Fazang* had declared closed, supplying three new names after Siṃha: Basiasita (25), Punyamitra (26), Prajñātāra (27) — then Bodhidharma (28).
- Competing Tang attempts to bridge the same gap disagree with each other most exactly at the bridge: the *Lidai Fabao Ji* (c. 775) offers its own resolution, Zongmi's (780–841) handling differs again, and modern analysis of the Tang lists — the “Missing Link” study of Siṃha's fate — confirms the names between Siṃha and Bodhidharma are the least stable segment of the entire list: minor variation for patriarchs 1–23, maximal variation for 24–27.
- **Does Prajñātāra's position survive contact with the earlier list? No.** He is not a figure the earlier list lost; he is an insertion at the seam, one of three names first attested precisely where the chart needed an Indian teacher for Bodhidharma and the inherited source said the line was extinct.

### 7.4 The gender question

Every pre-modern Chinese source places Prajñātāra inside an all-male patriarch list; classical Chinese frequently leaves gender unmarked, and no pre-modern text or identified depiction presents him as a woman. The *Jingde* biography's details — orphan, Keyura, Mahāsthāmaprāpta incarnation — carry no female marking.

The female identification is modern. It was proposed in print by Rev. Koten Benson (2008), on three grounds: claimed oral traditions in Korean Seon and in Kerala identifying Prajñātāra as female; the name (Prajñā + Tārā, both female deities); and archaeological evidence for famous female teachers in south India generally — real, but not evidence about this person. Practitioner literature adds a claim that Dōgen's *Shōbōgenzō* ch. 50 refers to Hannyatara as female; that claim was not verified against the text in this build and is not accepted in mainstream scholarship.

The grade is **modern conjecture (2008)**. Mainstream scholarly reaction ranges from dismissal to “possible but unevidenced.” The narrower honest statement is that the pre-modern record does not establish his gender because it barely establishes him; a tradition that cannot document the person cannot be overturned on the person's sex.

### 7.5 Prajñātāra — verdict rows

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Historical person | **Unproven; default = lineage construction at the seam.** Zero attestation before 801, zero attestation outside Chan lineage literature ever, inserted at the exact point where the inherited list (472) said the transmission had ended |
| Bodhidharma's teacher | **Late accretion** (801/952 against Bodhidharma's death ≤534: a gap of ~270–420 years) |
| Floruit | **None independent.** He inherits Bodhidharma's arithmetic entirely: if Bodhidharma (fl. c. 480–530) had an Indian teacher, that teacher's floruit falls c. 440–490 — arithmetic on a hypothesis, not a date. If the Guṇabhadra link is office-succession rather than pupilage (§5.2), Prajñātāra need not even be Guṇabhadra's contemporary |
| The gem test, renaming, 67-year prophecy | **Hagiography, first extant 1004** — narrative machinery for welding the chart's two halves |
| A woman | **Modern conjecture (2008)** — oral-tradition claims plus onomastics; no pre-modern evidence either way |

## 8. Modern scholarship map

- **Broughton (1999, *The Bodhidharma Anthology*):** translates the earliest stratum — the Dunhuang *Two Entrances* materials and Tanlin's preface — and treats it as the closest recoverable core: a real meditation master in the Luoyang region in the early 6th century, around whom an “anthology” of sayings crystallised. His dating of the Luoyang sighting (516–526) is the load-bearing terminus of §5.
- **McRae (1986; *Seeing Through Zen*, 2003):** the lineage is retrospective construction; his maxim is that lineage assertions are “as wrong as they are strong.” The patriarch chart is ideology in genealogical form, and Bodhidharma functions as a textual figure assembled over centuries by the factions that cited him.
- **Faure (1991; 2003):** Bodhidharma's historical existence is “dim”; the figure is a rhetorical and hagiographic construction — down to the body itself, the blue eyes and the barbarian otherness — successively rebuilt by Chan factions, Song editors, and modern national traditions.
- **Ferguson (2000, *Zen's Chinese Heritage*):** the tradition's own voice — retells the received biography with caveats flagged; valuable as a record of what the lineage believes about itself, not as critical history.
- **Dumoulin:** accepts a historical South Indian monk of the 5th–6th century behind the legend; doubtful of the Persian label; treats the wall-contemplation reputation as rooted in the man's actual practice profile.
- **Japanese scholarship:** Yanagida Seizan's Dunhuang source-criticism reconstructed early Chan from the manuscripts and exposed the Laṅkāvatāra school milieu that manufactured the first charts; Sekiguchi Shindai pressed radical doubts about the *Two Entrances* attribution itself. The terms of the modern debate were set earlier, in the 1930s Hu Shih – D. T. Suzuki exchange: Hu Shih read the texts as datable documents of a historical movement; Suzuki defended the tradition's reading. Suzuki's own historical judgement is worth recording against the tradition he defended: the 28-patriarch scheme was constructed when Chan, grown popular, was attacked for having “no transmission” — the pedigree answers a polemic.

**Consensus.** Modern scholarship agrees on the shape of the answer even where it splits on the man: a historical meditation master from the western regions — most plausibly South Indian by the earliest biographical layer, Persian/Central Asian by the earliest witness of all — was active in north China in the first decades of the 6th century, taught a contemplative method associated with the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, gathered at least two named disciples, and died before 534; everything beyond that kernel — the royal birth, the imperial audience, the Shaolin decade, the severed arm, the empty tomb, the Indian teacher, and the 28-patriarch line itself — was deposited in dated layers between the 7th and the 11th centuries, and, for the martial arts, the 17th, by a tradition building its own pedigree. The split is over the kernel's size: Broughton and Dumoulin keep a recoverable teacher at the centre; McRae and Faure treat even the centre as mostly textual effect. Nobody working from the dated sources defends the received biography, and nobody needs Bodhidharma to be a fiction — the Chinese record is sufficient to have contained the man; it is simply insufficient to contain the legend, which is how we know the legend is later.

## 9. Verdicts

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Did the man exist? | **Yes — high confidence.** Two independent near-contemporary witnesses (a non-sectarian gazetteer, 547; a disciple-line preface, 6th c.) with no shared motive to invent him, plus a sober first biography (Daoxuan) whose errors are the ordinary kind |
| When did he flourish? | **c. 480–530 CE.** Hard points: present in Luoyang 516–526 (Yang Xuanzhi, via Yongning Temple's 516 construction and 534 destruction); dead before 534. Arrival in China best supported under the Liu Song, by the 470s, per Daoxuan — the only arrival tradition compatible with the Guṇabhadra joint and the Luoyang terminus. The famous 527 arrival is falsified — by ~1–11 years against Yang, by ≥59 years against Guṇabhadra's death, and by ~8 years against its own text's Song Yun story |
| Where was he from? | **Western Regions — best-supported answer: South India, confidence moderate; Persian/Central Asian, confidence live.** The earliest witness says Persian *hu*; the next two say South Indian. The rule that the record closest to the event governs what it directly records keeps the Persian label permanently on the table; the South-Indian reading wins on the weight of the biographical tradition, not on priority. Royal birth: unproven. Pallava prince: modern conjecture, no Indian-side evidence |
| Which famous stories are early? | The 150-year age claim, as a claim (547); wall contemplation as a method name (6th c.); the Laṅkāvatāra affiliation and Huike's discipleship (by 667); residence on Mt. Song (by 728) |
| Which are later charters for the lineage? | Emperor Wu dialogue (758 — Shenhui's faction); Prajñātāra and the 28-patriarch line (801 — pedigree under polemical pressure); the 527 arrival, single sandal, empty tomb (952 — Five Dynasties consolidation); nine years at Shaolin, self-severed arm, reed crossing, poisonings, gem test, 67-year prophecy (1004 — Song canonisation); martial arts (1624 — forgery) |
| Prajñātāra | **Lineage construction at the seam** (§7.5). No attestation before 801, none outside Chan lineage literature, inserted where the 472 list had closed the line |

## 10. Gaps and limits

Two gaps are declared in the source build, and both qualify claims made in this paper.

1. **The “blue-eyed barbarian” first occurrence is not pinned.** The exact first dated occurrence of the epithet “blue-eyed barbarian” (碧眼胡) was not pinned in this build. It is standard by the Song lamp literature and is graded here as attested by 1004, earlier occurrence unverified. No earlier date for the epithet is claimed in this paper.
2. **The *Baolin Zhuan* is lost.** The *Baolin Zhuan* (801), which fixed the 28-patriarch list, is lost and known through quotation, so anything credited to it — including Prajñātāra's first attestation in 801 and the fixing of the list itself — is one notch softer than an extant text.

Beyond those two, the build's stated limits are preserved rather than smoothed over. Where a detail comes only from secondary summary and could not be checked against the text itself, it is flagged in place — as with the “over 180” age figure in secondary summaries of Daoxuan (§2.3) and the practitioner claim about Dōgen's *Shōbōgenzō* ch. 50 (§7.4). The dossier rests on the primary witnesses as reported in the standard scholarship named in §1, not on a fresh collation of every witness. The verdict on the man's existence rests on two near-contemporary witnesses, and its confidence is held at exactly that strength (§11, item 5).

## 11. What would change these verdicts

1. **Any pre-801 attestation of Prajñātāra** — a Dunhuang fragment, a catalogue entry, a Tibetan translation naming him as Bodhidharma's teacher — would move him from “construction” to “tradition with an early root,” and force re-grading of the whole Indian half of the chart.
2. **Any Indian-side attestation of Bodhidharma** — an inscription, a Sanskrit or Tamil text, a Pallava-era record of a prince-monk departing for China — would transform the origin question overnight and give the Indian half of the chart its first cross-datable node.
3. **A dated 6th-century Chinese document naming Bodhidharma's teacher, origin, or arrival year** — a tomb inscription, a monastery record, a dated colophon — would collapse the floruit interval to a point and arbitrate the Persian-versus-South-Indian conflict with evidence instead of witness-weighting.
4. **Recovery of the *Baolin Zhuan* text** would let the 801 layer be read instead of inferred, and would date, or dissolve, the gem-test and prophecy motifs currently credited to 1004 by default.
5. Conversely, **a pre-534 source placing a different master behind the *Two Entrances* attribution** would shrink the historical kernel toward McRae's and Faure's end of the consensus — the verdict's confidence in the man rests on two witnesses, and it is honestly held at exactly that strength.
